Technical note

Reading between the lines

Measuring patient experience from unsolicited feedback — and what happened when I tried to break my own sentiment model.

Kaycee Ndukuba MSc Applied Data Science · BSc Psychology Three interactive exercises · 5 min

Turning unsolicited patient comments into a comparable score is, in the language of my first degree, a construct validation problem: the claim that something cheap and instantaneous tracks something expensive and slow. That is the same argument psychometrics has been having for a century, and it is why I think about text models the way I do.

Three notes on that problem. Each one has a short exercise, because these points are easier to experience than to be told. Every model figure below is real and reproducible.

01One comment is not one score

A single comment routinely touches several domains at once — and, more awkwardly, can be positive about one and negative about another in the same breath.

Exercise 1 · Tag the domains

"Arrived at 7am for a 9am appointment and wasn't seen until half one. Nobody told us what was happening or how long it would be. The nurse who finally took my bloods was lovely — she explained everything properly and couldn't have been kinder."

Which domains does this comment speak to? Tick all that apply.

This comment is four labels, with two different polarities. A single overall sentiment score averages it to roughly neutral and destroys everything actionable in it: the access failure and the communication failure both disappear, and so does the fact that the clinical interaction was genuinely good.

That is why domain assignment has to be a set of binary relevance decisions rather than one seven-way choice, with sentiment scored per domain. It also means evaluation has to be per-domain — a headline average lets a frequent domain quietly carry a rare one.

The 1–5 score compounds this. It is ordinal — the gap between 1 and 5 is not the same as the gap between 4 and 5 — so standard classification is blind to the ordering and penalises a near-miss exactly as hard as a total miss. An ordinal formulation evaluated with quadratic weighted kappa or mean absolute error respects that difference.

02What happened when I tested my own assumption

I built a three-class sentiment classifier on 14,640 real airline customer messages — the closest public analogue I have to unsolicited service feedback. It reaches 79.3% accuracy and 0.721 macro F1 on a held-out split. I report macro F1 rather than accuracy because the data skews heavily negative, as every complaints channel does, and accuracy would flatter a model that simply guessed the majority class.

The pipeline extracts structural features from the raw text before cleaning — capitalisation, exclamation and question marks, emoji. I had written that these gave the model purchase on tone, and therefore on sarcasm. Then it occurred to me I had asserted that without ever testing it.

Exercise 2 · The ablation

Same sentence, tone markers switched on and off. If my claim were right, removing them should make the model less sure it's negative.

P(negative) — real output from the deployed model

The prediction moves the wrong way. Stripping the tone markers made the model more confident, not less. The structural features were not carrying that example — the words were, and they did a cleaner job without the markers muddying them. My claim was wrong.

So I pushed harder, with sentences whose surface wording is uniformly positive and only the intent is negative.

Exercise 3 · Can you beat the model?

Five sarcastic sentences. Mark each one as a human would read it, then see what the model said.

Both successes contain negative lexical content that leaks through — worst enemy, losing my luggage. The three failures are uniformly positive on the surface. On the last one the model is 99% confident and completely wrong.

Why this happens

Bag-of-words has no mechanism for incongruity. Sarcasm is a contradiction between a positive expression and a negative situation, and a representation that treats a sentence as an unordered collection of tokens has nothing that can be in tension with anything else. Counting exclamation marks does not fix it, because the marker is not the meaning. The remedy is a contextual encoder, where love and stuck on the tarmac can inform one another.

The failure that matters more

Note that an abstention threshold would not have caught the worst case. The model was 99% confident and wrong, so a confidence cut-off never fires. That is a calibration failure, distinct from an accuracy failure — and where a score may trigger an intervention, it is the more consequential of the two. Reliability curves and expected calibration error belong next to the headline metric, not after it.

03The label ceiling, and who bothers to write

A model trained on expertly annotated comments inherits whatever agreement those annotators had. If two trained humans reading the same sentence disagree on whether it reflects emotional support, no amount of modelling recovers a ground truth that was never there. Inter-annotator agreement is a ceiling on achievable performance, not a footnote — Cohen's kappa for two raters, Krippendorff's alpha where coverage is uneven.

The useful part is diagnostic: the domains where annotators disagree most are the domains where the construct is least well defined, which tells you where the definition needs work before the model does. My untested prior is that emotional support is hardest of the seven, because it depends most on inference about what a patient meant rather than what they said.

Separately, unsolicited feedback over-represents the extremes and skews by age, digital access and condition type — and often the patients whose experience matters most are least likely to post. Validation against an established survey is the substantive answer to that. The question I would want to understand is where it holds and where it thins: how alignment behaves in low-volume subgroups, and whether location-level scores carry uncertainty alongside them. A score built on nine comments and one built on nine hundred should not look identical on a dashboard, because a hospital will act on both as if they were.

04What I would look at first

  • The annotation guidelines and the agreement statistics, before touching a model. You cannot improve a measurement whose definition you have not read.
  • Where the residual error concentrates per domain — and whether one domain is simply harder than the rest.
  • Calibration, not only accuracy, for any score that drives an intervention.
  • A client conversation, early. I don't think the write-up can be written well by someone who has never heard the question being asked.
