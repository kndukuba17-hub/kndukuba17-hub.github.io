Technical note

Reading between the lines

Measuring patient experience from unsolicited feedback: what a sentiment model gets right, where it breaks, and why the difference between those two decides what a score is safe to act on.

Kaycee Ndukuba MSc Applied Data Science · BSc Psychology 6 min read

Turning unsolicited patient comments into a comparable score is, in the language of my first degree, a construct validation problem: the claim that something cheap and instantaneous tracks something expensive and slow. That is the argument psychometrics has been having for a century, and it is why I think about text models the way I do.

What follows is four observations about that problem. The evidence is drawn from a sentiment classifier I trained on 14,640 real airline customer messages — the closest public analogue I have to unsolicited service feedback — and deployed to AWS Lambda. Every figure below is real output from that model and reproducible from source.

01One comment is not one score

A single comment routinely speaks to several domains at once — and, more awkwardly, can be positive about one and negative about another in the same breath.

"Arrived at 7am for a 9am appointment and wasn't seen until half one. Nobody told us what was happening or how long it would be. The nurse who finally took my bloods was lovely — she explained everything properly and couldn't have been kinder."

So domain assignment is a set of binary relevance decisions rather than one seven-way choice, with sentiment scored per domain. Evaluation has to follow: a headline average lets a frequent domain quietly carry a rare one.

The 1–5 score compounds this, because it is ordinal. The gap between 1 and 5 is not the gap between 4 and 5, but standard classification is blind to that and penalises a near-miss exactly as hard as a total miss. An ordinal formulation evaluated with quadratic weighted kappa or mean absolute error respects the difference.

02What the model gets right, and why the errors lean the right way

The job that matters in a feedback pipeline is catching the unhappy patient. On that specific job this model is strong: it identifies 91.6% of all negative messages (F1 0.875 on that class). If the purpose is routing complaints to a human, missing fewer than one in ten is the number that counts.

The error structure is the more interesting part, because errors are not symmetrical in their cost.

The caveat that comes with it

That same bias becomes a liability the moment the output is used as a measure rather than a queue. A system that leans toward "negative" will inflate an organisation's apparent complaint volume, and a trust comparing itself year on year would read a model artefact as a real decline. The same behaviour is a feature for routing and a bug for benchmarking — which is an argument for calibrating the decision threshold separately for each use, rather than shipping one score for both.

03Why I report macro F1 and not accuracy

Feedback channels are lopsided. Mine is 63% negative, which is entirely normal — people write when something went wrong.

04Checking a claim I had made

The pipeline extracts structural features from the raw text before cleaning — capitalisation, exclamation and question marks, emoji. I had written that these gave the model purchase on tone, and therefore on sarcasm. Then it occurred to me that I had asserted it without ever testing it.

So I ran an ablation: take one sarcastic sentence, strip the tone markers, and watch what happens. If the claim held, confidence should fall.

So I pushed harder, with sentences whose surface wording is uniformly positive and only the intent is negative.

Why this happens

Bag-of-words has no mechanism for incongruity. Sarcasm is a contradiction between a positive expression and a negative situation, and a representation that treats a sentence as an unordered collection of tokens has nothing that can be in tension with anything else. Counting exclamation marks does not repair that, because the marker is not the meaning. The remedy is a contextual encoder, where love and stuck on the tarmac can inform one another.

05What confidence does and does not tell you

The obvious response to the above is an abstention threshold — route anything the model is unsure about to a human. That only works if confidence means something, so I checked.

The finding that matters

And yet it was 99% confident and wrong on "thanks for the amazing 5 hour delay". Both things are true at once, and the tension between them is the point: aggregate calibration is not a safety guarantee. It is measured on data drawn from the same distribution as training, so it says nothing about the input that does not resemble it. An abstention threshold tuned on that curve would never have fired on the sentence that mattered most.

Which is why, for any score that triggers an intervention, I would want calibration reported alongside accuracy and a deliberate adversarial set — not because it improves the headline number, but because it is the only thing that finds this class of failure.

06The label ceiling, and who bothers to write

A model trained on expertly annotated comments inherits whatever agreement those annotators had. If two trained people reading the same sentence disagree on whether it reflects emotional support, no amount of modelling recovers a ground truth that was never there. Inter-annotator agreement is a ceiling on achievable performance, not a footnote — Cohen's kappa for two raters, Krippendorff's alpha where coverage is uneven.

The useful part is diagnostic. The domains where annotators disagree most are the domains where the construct is least well defined, which tells you where the definition needs work before the model does. My untested prior is that emotional support is hardest of the seven, because it depends most on inference about what a patient meant rather than what they said.

Separately, unsolicited feedback over-represents the extremes and skews by age, digital access and condition type — and often the patients whose experience matters most are least likely to post. Validation against an established survey is the substantive answer to that. What I would want to understand is where it holds and where it thins: how alignment behaves in low-volume subgroups, and whether location-level scores carry uncertainty alongside them. A score built on nine comments and one built on nine hundred should not look identical on a dashboard, because a hospital will act on both as though they were.

07What I would look at first

  • The annotation guidelines and the agreement statistics, before touching a model. You cannot improve a measurement whose definition you have not read.
  • The confusion structure per domain — not the headline accuracy, but which specific confusions dominate, and whether they are directional in a way that would mislead a client.
  • Calibration and an adversarial set for any score that drives an intervention.
  • A client conversation, early. I don't think the write-up can be written well by someone who has never heard the question being asked.

Notes on sources and method

The patient comment in §1 is an illustrative composite written for this note, not a real review. The seven domain names follow the framing used in published patient-experience measurement.

All model figures are real output from a three-class sentiment classifier trained on the Twitter US Airline Sentiment dataset (14,640 messages; stratified 10% held-out split) and are reproducible from source: python -m src.train_export and python -m src.sarcasm_probe. The model is deployed to AWS Lambda as a container image with CloudWatch monitoring.

Charts are hand-built SVG with no plotting library. The two-colour encoding was validated for protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia rather than chosen by eye, and every categorical distinction is carried by a text label as well as by colour.
